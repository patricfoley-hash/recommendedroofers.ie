How do I know if my Dublin gutter needs a repair or a replacement?

Three faults account for most Dublin gutter calls, and telling them apart matters because the right answer is different for each. A blockage or a lost fall dams the run at one point and the overflow is local to that spot. A failed joint or bracket drips behind the gutter onto the fascia, so the symptom is a wet stripe on the board rather than water pouring over the front. And on many older Dublin houses the profile is simply too small for the roof area, so the whole run overflows along its length even when it is perfectly clean.

The first two are repairs. The third is a replacement, usually to a 125mm deep-flow profile, because no amount of resealing a 112mm half-round will fix a gutter that is undersized for the roof it serves. A run that sags between every bracket, cracks along its length, or has more patches than gutter has also stopped being economical to repair.

Dublin's housing stock predicts the fault more often than not. Georgian and Victorian redbrick in Rathmines, Phibsborough, Drumcondra, Portobello and Ranelagh runs on original cast iron that is often worth saving, with rust that has pitted the surface repairable and rust through the base of the section not. The 1930s and 1940s semis in Cabra, Crumlin, Terenure and Clontarf have uPVC fitted decades ago that is now brittle from UV, with joint seals hardened past the point of resealing. The 1970s estates in Tallaght, Clondalkin, Coolock and Ballymun carry the standard 112mm half-round that is frequently undersized and overflows even when clean. Newer developments in Citywest, Clonsilla and Adamstown are generally sound, with blockages and the occasional bracket.

Coastal exposure matters too. Howth, Sutton, Malahide, Clontarf, Sandymount and Dun Laoghaire take wind loading that inland areas do not, so bracket spacing matters more there and a run that holds in Tallaght will pull away on an exposed coastal gable.
