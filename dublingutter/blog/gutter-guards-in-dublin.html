Gutter Guards in Dublin

Costs 11 min read 2026-10-20 By Patrick Foley, Business Owner

Gutter guards are worth fitting on Dublin homes only where mature trees overhang the roof and ladder access is genuinely difficult — most Rathmines, Ranelagh and Terenure houses under London plane, lime or sycamore fit that description. Everywhere else, a scheduled clear costs less and works better. Expect €15–€35 per metre supplied and fitted, indicative and confirmed after a survey.

Summary

  • Under London plane, lime and sycamore in Rathmines, Ranelagh, Terenure and the Phoenix Park fringe, two clears a year is the baseline whether guards are fitted or not.
  • Mesh and brush guards on Dublin's standard 112mm half-round profiles typically cost €15–€35 per metre supplied and fitted (indicative — confirmed after a survey).
  • Plane tree seed balls and lime tree sap are the two things that defeat almost every guard sold in Ireland — they clog mesh and glue brushes solid.
  • Foam inserts in ogee gutters trap silt and moss above the outlet and cause overflow behind the fascia, which is worse than the blockage they replaced.
  • A standard gutter clear on a Dublin semi is priced per job with no call-out fee, and clears the downpipes as part of the same visit.
  • Guards do not remove the need for cleaning; they change what you clean and how often you have to get up there to do it.

What actually falls into a Dublin gutter under a plane tree

The honest starting point is that "leaves" is the smallest part of the problem on the leafy roads of south Dublin. On a mature London plane — the dominant street tree along Palmerston Road, Kenilworth Square, Grosvenor Road and much of Ranelagh — the material that clogs gutters through the year is:

  • Seed balls from October through March. Roughly 25–30mm across, fibrous, and they break down into an irritant fluff that mats into anything with a mesh finer than 4mm.
  • Bark plates shed from May onwards. These are the flat, jigsaw-piece flakes that give the plane its patchy trunk. They land whole, sit flat over an outlet and dam the run in a single storm.
  • Leaves in November, which everyone expects and which are the easiest of the three to deal with.

Lime trees — common through Terenure, Rathgar and the roads bordering the Phoenix Park — add a fourth problem: aphid honeydew. It coats leaves and gutter surfaces in a sticky film that sets brush-style guards solid within one season. Sycamores, prevalent in older Ranelagh gardens, drop keys that wedge into any perforated guard and then rot in place.

None of this is theoretical. It is the difference between a guard that earns its place and one that makes the annual gutter cleaning call harder rather than unnecessary.

When gutter guards genuinely help

There is a narrow set of Dublin situations where fitting guards is the right call rather than the upsell:

  • A three-storey Victorian on Palmerston Road with a plane directly overhead and no rear lane access. Getting a ladder or tower to the front elevation is a scheduled operation, not a fifteen-minute visit. Guards here cut the risk of a mid-winter blockage between clears.
  • A dormer or bay above a conservatory roof where the gutter above the glass is effectively unreachable without dismantling the conservatory. Keeping large debris out of that specific run is worth the compromise.
  • A house with a shared valley gutter between two roof planes that catches everything and is awkward to reach — common in the terraced stock around Portobello and parts of Rathmines.
  • Any run where the fall is short and the outlet is at the awkward end relative to the prevailing drop of leaves.

In each of these, the guard is not eliminating maintenance. It is changing a full clear into a top-brush and a check of the outlets.

When guards make things worse

This is the part the retail brochures skip. On a standard Dublin half-round or ogee gutter, the wrong guard is worse than no guard, and here is how the failure looks:

Foam inserts in ogee profiles. The foam absorbs moss spores and windborne silt, holds them, and turns into a saturated sponge that sits above the outlet. Water then finds the fascia rather than the downpipe. On the 1930s and 1940s semis through Terenure and Rathgar, where the fascia board is usually the original timber, this rots the board out inside two winters. The repair is not the guard — it is a full fascia and soffit renewal with the guttering off in one visit.

Fine mesh under plane trees. Anything finer than about 4mm aperture clogs with plane seed fluff and lime honeydew. The blockage forms on top of the mesh rather than inside the gutter, which sounds better until you realise water then sheets off the front of the gutter in every heavy shower, staining the render and eroding the pointing below the eaves.

Brush inserts under lime trees. The honeydew sets the bristles into a solid mat within one summer. Removing them to clean the gutter beneath means pulling them out saturated and often refitting new — an ongoing cost that no one flagged at the quote stage.

Clip-in plastic covers on 112mm half-round. These are what most 1970s estates through Tallaght, Clondalkin and Coolock end up with when a well-meaning neighbour recommends "the ones from the DIY shop". They lift in high wind, they warp in summer sun, and they still need lifting to clear the outlet — which is where the blockage always is anyway.

What Dublin's housing stock means for the guard question

The address usually predicts whether guards make sense, because it predicts the gutter profile and the trees around it:

  • Georgian and Victorian redbrick (Rathmines, Ranelagh, Portobello, Phibsborough, Drumcondra). Original cast iron in ogee or half-round, tree-lined streets, three storeys plus. Guards worth considering on the front elevation where access is hard; the rear is usually clearable off a normal ladder and does not need them.
  • 1930s and 1940s semis (Terenure, Rathgar, Cabra, Clontarf). uPVC that has been up for decades, brittle in places, with fascia timber that has already softened. Fitting guards to a system that is close to needing full gutter replacement is money spent twice.
  • 1970s estates (Tallaght, Clondalkin, Coolock). 112mm half-round, often undersized for the roof area, on open ground with few mature trees. Guards solve a problem these houses do not have; the issue is profile size, not debris.
  • Newer developments (Citywest, Clonsilla, Adamstown). Generally sound, few trees, calls here are one-off blockages. Guards are almost never the right answer.

A standard three-bed semi carries roughly 15–20 metres of gutter front and rear combined, so a mid-spec mesh guard across the whole house lands somewhere between €300 and €600 fitted, on top of whatever the gutter itself needs. Note the "on top of" — guards do not extend the life of a tired uPVC run, and fitting them to a system that needs gutter repair first is the most common mis-spend we see.

For context, a scheduled clear on the same house — priced per job, no call-out fee — is typically well under the annual cost of amortising a full set of guards over their realistic lifespan under a plane tree. The maths only tips in favour of guards where access is the problem, not debris volume.

The Rathmines and Ranelagh reality check

Two clears a year is the baseline on any road with mature London plane, lime or sycamore within about fifteen metres of the roof. That includes most of the numbered roads off Rathmines Road Lower, most of Ranelagh village and the residential streets running south towards Milltown, all of Palmerston and Kenilworth, and the Phoenix Park fringe from Chapelizod through Castleknock.

With guards fitted, you are still up there twice a year — once to top-brush and check outlets in November, once in spring to clear whatever the winter storms drove onto the mesh. Without guards, you are up there twice to actually clear the run. The time difference on a semi is maybe forty minutes across the year. The cost difference over a decade, with guards priced honestly and clears priced honestly, is close to a wash on most houses and favours the clear-only approach on the majority.

The exception, and it is a real one, is the tall Victorian with a plane directly overhead and no side access. There, guards buy you insurance against the mid-January blockage that turns into water down a chimney breast. That is worth paying for.

What we actually recommend

We do not fit gutter guards as a default upsell. On most Dublin houses, a clear on a schedule is cheaper and works better, and we say so at the quote. When we do recommend guards, it is a specific mesh product on a specific elevation for a specific access reason, priced with the reasoning written down.

Where the gutter itself is at the end of its life — brittle uPVC, hardened joint seals, brackets pulling through soft fascia — the honest answer is a full replacement in a properly sized profile, not a guard fitted to a system that will fail underneath it.
