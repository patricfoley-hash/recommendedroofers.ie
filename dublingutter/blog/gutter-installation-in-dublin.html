Gutter Installation in Dublin

Costs 9 min read 2026-11-13 By Patrick Foley, Business Owner

New gutter installation in Dublin costs from €1,000 for a standard three-bed semi in uPVC, rising to €5,500–€6,500 for cast iron on a larger period property. Most jobs take one to two days with directly employed crews, include new brackets, unions, outlets and downpipes, and carry a 10-year written guarantee alongside a Lifetime Leak Guarantee on full replacements.

Summary

  • Indicative installed rates: €50–€65 per metre for uPVC, €65–€75 for aluminium, €75–€90 for cast iron — supplied and fitted, including brackets, unions and outlets.
  • A typical Dublin semi carries 15–20 metres of guttering front and rear combined, so uPVC lands around €1,000–€1,400 and aluminium €1,300–€1,800 installed.
  • Duration: most single-house installations complete in one to two working days; larger detached properties or cast iron restorations take longer.
  • Profile matters more than material on 1970s estates like Tallaght, Clondalkin and Coolock, where original 112mm half-round is often undersized and needs upsizing to 125mm deep-flow.
  • Coastal Dublin needs tighter bracket spacing — Howth, Sutton, Malahide, Sandymount and Dun Laoghaire take wind loading that inland runs do not.
  • Every full installation carries both a 10-year written workmanship guarantee and a Lifetime Leak Guarantee, handed over in writing before contracts are signed.

How much does gutter installation cost in Dublin?

Price is driven by four things every time: the length of run, the material chosen, the height and access, and whether the fascia and soffit behind the gutter are sound enough to hold new brackets. A written breakdown after a free survey is the only way to be exact, but the indicative ranges below place most Dublin jobs.

Material is the biggest lever. The materials alone move from roughly €10 per metre for uPVC up to €50 per metre for cast iron, and on a 50-metre run that difference is around €3,000 before any labour is added.

Which material should you fit — uPVC, aluminium or cast iron?

Match the material to the house, not to the cheapest quote.

uPVC is the default across most of Dublin's 1930s–1990s stock and the correct choice for the majority of semis. Well-fitted uPVC should give thirty years or more, comes in black, brown, grey and white in the common profiles, and is straightforward to match to what neighbours have.

Aluminium earns its premium where wind loading is a factor — exposed coastal gables in Howth, Sutton and Malahide, or three-storey properties where getting up to the roofline is a job in itself. It doesn't go brittle from UV the way uPVC eventually does.

Cast iron belongs on Georgian and Victorian redbrick — Rathmines, Phibsborough, Drumcondra, Portobello, Ranelagh — where uPVC on the front elevation looks wrong and, in a conservation area, may not be permitted. On a period house original cast iron is often worth repairing in matching sections rather than replaced wholesale. How long does a gutter installation take?

Most single-house installations are a one-to-two-day job with a directly employed crew. A straightforward uPVC replacement on a three-bed semi — front and rear, new downpipes, new outlets — is typically finished inside a day. Add fascia and soffit renewal, or move to aluminium or cast iron, and it stretches into a second day.

The sequence on site runs the same way every time:

  1. Old guttering, downpipes and any failed board removed and taken away.
  2. Fascia checked and, where the timber has softened, replaced before anything new is fixed.
  3. New brackets set to a correct fall — roughly 3mm per metre toward the outlet.
  4. Gutter, unions and outlets fitted, then downpipes plumbed to the existing gullies.
  5. The full run is flushed with water so we know it clears — no guessing.
  6. Written guarantee handed over on the day.

Do you need to replace fascia and soffit at the same time?

Often yes, and it's cheaper done together than as two separate visits. Where the fascia board a gutter is screwed to has gone soft — common on 1930s and 1940s semis in Cabra, Crumlin, Terenure and Clontarf — there is nothing for a new bracket to bite into. Fitting new gutter to rotten board buys a season, not a decade.

We check the timber during the survey. If the board is sound, you keep the guttering-only price. If it isn't, you get the combined figure in writing before anything is agreed. Our fascia and soffit installation work is priced with the guttering when both are done in one visit, because the gutter has to come off to reach the board anyway.

What size and profile of gutter do you actually need?

This is where a lot of Dublin overflow problems start, and it's the question a driveway estimate can't answer.

Standard 112mm half-round is the default on most housing, but on 1970s estates — Tallaght, Clondalkin, Coolock, Ballymun — it's frequently undersized for the roof area it drains. The tell is a run that overflows along its whole length even when it's just been cleared. That isn't a repair problem. It's a capacity problem, and the fix is upsizing to a 125mm deep-flow profile with matched outlets and downpipes.

Georgian and Victorian houses come with their own logic. Original cast iron ogee profiles carried water for a hundred years and were sized for the roofs they were made for. Where cast iron is being replaced like-for-like, matching the profile matters both hydraulically and visually.

What about coastal exposure and wind loading?

Bracket spacing is the thing that separates a good coastal installation from one that pulls away in the first winter storm. Howth, Sutton, Malahide, Clontarf, Sandymount and Dun Laoghaire all take gable-end wind loading that inland Dublin does not.

The standard is a bracket every metre. On exposed coastal elevations we tighten that — typically 600–800mm centres, sometimes closer on a two-storey gable that catches an easterly. Aluminium is often the better choice here because it holds its shape under load without flexing at the joints.

Is planning permission required for new guttering?

For like-for-like replacement — same material, same profile, same colour — no permission is needed. For a house in a designated Architectural Conservation Area, or a protected structure, changing from cast iron to uPVC on a street-facing elevation may require permission from the local authority. Dublin City Council, Dún Laoghaire-Rathdown, Fingal and South Dublin each publish their own ACA lists. If you're unsure, we check before quoting rather than after.

How to choose a Dublin gutter installer

Four things worth checking, whoever you're getting quotes from:

  • Directly employed crews vs. subcontracted labour. The subcontract model is why so many guttering jobs have no comeback when they leak the following winter. Ask.
  • Insurance in writing. €6.5M public liability and €13M employer liability is the level we carry; ask to see certificates before appointing anyone.
  • CIRI registration. The Construction Industry Register Ireland is the state-backed register. Members are checkable.
  • The guarantee, before signing. A verbal "we'll stand over it" is not a guarantee. Get the terms — length, scope, what triggers a callback — in writing before contracts are signed.

We're a member of the National Guild of Master Craftsmen and Guaranteed Irish, hold CIRI registration, and hand the 10-year written guarantee and Lifetime Leak Guarantee across before the job starts, not after. For the fuller credential list see the Dublin guttering services page.

What happens after installation?

New guttering that's correctly sized and correctly fitted needs one clear a year in late autumn once the leaves are down. Two clears a year is worth it on leafier roads — Rathgar, Terenure, Clontarf, Mount Merrion, Castleknock — where mature trees within about fifteen metres will fill a gutter faster than a single visit can keep pace with.

We don't fit gutter guards as a default upsell. On most Dublin houses a scheduled gutter cleaning visit is cheaper and works better; guards keep leaves out but trap silt and moss, and still have to be cleaned. Where access is genuinely difficult under heavy overhanging trees, guards earn their place.
