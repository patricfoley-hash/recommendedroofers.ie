Downpipes and Drainage in Dublin

Costs 11 min read 2026-09-22

On most Dublin houses the downpipe discharges into a trapped gully at the base of the wall, which connects to either a combined sewer (Georgian and Victorian terraces, inner city) or a separate surface-water drain (post-1970s estates). Pooling and damp at the wall base almost always mean the gully is blocked, cracked, or the pipe is discharging beside it rather than into it.

The short version

  • Roughly 15–20 metres of gutter on a standard Dublin three-bed semi feeds two to four downpipes, each carrying the runoff from around 30–50 m² of roof.
  • Pre-1970s inner-city terraces are on combined sewers — one pipe carries both foul waste and rainwater to the Ringsend treatment works, which is why Uisce Éireann restricts new surface-water connections into it.
  • Newer estates from the 1970s onward are typically on separate systems, where the downpipe must go to the surface-water drain and never into the foul.
  • A trapped gully with a 100mm water seal is what stops sewer smells rising back up the downpipe on a combined system — a dry or cracked gully is a common cause of drain odour at the front door.
  • Damp at the base of the wall within 300mm of a downpipe points to the gully, the shoe, or the pipe joint above it — not to rising damp, and not to the gutter itself.
  • Gutter repair on a Dublin semi runs €500–€800 for a small job and €1,000–€1,900 for a typical downpipe-and-alignment fix (indicative — confirmed free after a survey).

Where the water goes when it leaves the roof

The gutter is the easy bit. The harder question — and the one that decides whether a wall stays dry — is what happens in the last two metres, between the shoe at the bottom of the downpipe and the ground.

On a Dublin house the water has one of three destinations:

  1. A trapped gully set into the path or the front garden, connected below ground to either a combined sewer or a surface-water drain.
  2. A back-inlet gully where the downpipe enters the drain below the grating, common on rear elevations and shared yards.
  3. A soakaway or channel drain on newer estates where the ground can take it, or where Uisce Éireann has required surface water to be dealt with on site.

Which one you have is almost entirely a function of when the house was built and where in the city it sits. That is where the diagnosis starts.

1930s and 1940s semis — Cabra, Crumlin, Terenure, Clontarf

The classic pattern here is a two-pipe front elevation feeding trapped gullies in the front garden, with a single rear downpipe into a back-inlet gully in the yard. The original cast-iron pipes have almost all been replaced with 68mm uPVC round or 65mm square profile over the last thirty years.

Two things go wrong repeatedly on this stock:

The gully cracks or silts up. The original clayware gullies are now ninety years old. Fine grit from the roof — moss, felt granules from a re-covered roof, wind-blown soil — builds up in the trap until water backs up and overflows onto the path. From there it tracks along the wall and shows up inside as damp on the skirting.

The shoe misses the grating. A downpipe that has been re-clipped over the years often ends 100–150mm off-centre from the gully below. In light rain the water finds the drain anyway. In a Dublin downpour — the kind that dropped 40mm in an hour in the November 2023 event — half of it lands on the concrete beside the gully and soaks straight into the wall.

The fix is usually a new shoe, a new bearing on the lowest bracket, and a proper rod-and-jet of the gully. If the clayware trap has cracked, the gully itself gets swapped for a modern PVC one with a removable silt bucket. Our gutter repairs page covers the typical scope; the downpipes section deals specifically with the shoe-to-gully alignment.

Georgian and Victorian terraces — Rathmines, Portobello, Phibsborough, Drumcondra

These are the awkward ones, and the ones where a bad diagnosis costs the most.

The front elevation is straightforward — usually a single cast-iron downpipe at the party wall, discharging to a gully at the base of the steps or into a hopper at basement level. It is the rear that causes the arguments. A Victorian terrace typically has a shared rear yard, a shared return, and drainage that runs under someone else's flags before it reaches the main sewer at the front.

A few things worth knowing before spending money:

  • The connection is almost always combined. Everything inside the old canal ring — foul, surface water, the lot — goes to one pipe and ends up at Ringsend. Uisce Éireann's stated position is that new surface-water connections into the combined network are not permitted where a separate outfall exists, but on Victorian stock a separate outfall usually does not exist, so the combined arrangement stays.
  • The gully at the base of the rear downpipe is the smell trap for the whole yard. If it dries out in summer — no rain for a fortnight in Rathmines — the water seal fails and sewer gas rises up the downpipe. The fix is a bucket of water down the gully once a fortnight in dry weather, not a new drain.
  • The party-wall downpipe is often shared. Cast-iron pipes on the boundary between two houses were sometimes installed as one pipe collecting both roofs via a hopper at eaves level. Repairs and replacements need agreement with the neighbour, and the cast iron on period property work carries a materials cost of around €50 per metre against €10 for uPVC.

Where a Victorian rear yard is pooling in heavy rain, the cause is usually one of three things: the shared gully is silted, the below-ground pipe has a root intrusion at a clay joint, or the yard's own gully — separate from the downpipe — has failed. Only the first is a roofline job. The second is drainage. The third is paving.

1970s estates — Tallaght, Clondalkin, Coolock, Ballymun

Separate systems from here on. The rainwater downpipe must go to the surface-water drain, and the foul stack goes to the foul. Cross-connections — a rainwater pipe plumbed into the foul, or a washing machine draining into the surface-water gully — are a genuine problem in this age band and one Uisce Éireann has been active on since the misconnections work in the Dodder and Camac catchments.

The typical failure here is different again. The 112mm half-round gutter on a 1970s semi is often undersized for the roof it serves, so in a heavy shower the gutter overflows along the whole run rather than at the outlet. The downpipe never gets the water in the first place. Owners call for a downpipe clear; what they actually need is a 125mm deep-flow upgrade, which is a full replacement on that elevation.

The gutter cleaning call sorts a blocked outlet. The overflow-along-the-run pattern is a sizing issue and a full replacement job.

Newer developments — Citywest, Adamstown, Clonsilla

Post-2000 estates are generally sound. Surface water is separated at the boundary, downpipes discharge to purpose-built gullies with silt buckets, and many sites have an on-plot soakaway or an attenuation tank that the downpipe feeds via a channel drain. The calls here are almost always blockages — a tennis ball in a hopper, a nesting bird in an offset — rather than design problems.

Two things to check on this stock before calling anyone:

  • The gully grating lifts off. Have a look. Nine times out of ten there is a mat of oak or sycamore leaves sitting on top of the silt bucket. Lift it out, empty it, put it back. That is the whole job.
  • The channel drain across the driveway is not the downpipe's drain. The downpipe has its own gully. If the channel drain is blocked, the driveway floods but the downpipe still runs.

Diagnosing damp at the base of the wall

This is the call we get most often in October and November, once the first sustained wet week hits. The pattern to test for, in order:

  1. Is the damp within 300mm of a downpipe? If yes, the downpipe or its gully is the first suspect, not rising damp.
  2. Does it worsen in heavy rain within an hour? Rising damp is slow and constant. Downpipe-related damp tracks the weather.
  3. Is there staining on the render or the brick above the gully? A splash pattern 200–400mm up the wall means water is leaving the pipe and hitting the wall rather than entering the gully.
  4. Does the gully hold water when you pour a bucket in? A working trap holds the water. A cracked trap drains it away within seconds — that is the fault.
  5. Is there moss or a green stain running down the wall from a joint in the pipe? A split at a socket joint, common on brittle uPVC in south- and west-facing elevations after twenty years of UV.

If four of the five point at the downpipe, it is a downpipe job. If none of them do, the damp is coming from somewhere else — a defective flashing, a cracked render, a leaking waste pipe — and a gutter contractor is the wrong first call.

What Uisce Éireann's rules mean for you in practice

The rules are simpler than they sound:

  • On a combined system (most pre-1970 inner Dublin), the existing downpipe-to-gully arrangement stays as it is. New work matches what is there.
  • On a separate system (most post-1970 estates), rainwater goes to the surface-water drain and only to the surface-water drain. A misconnection into the foul is a defect the seller has to disclose on a property sale.
  • New impermeable area — a paved-over front garden, an extension — can trigger a requirement for on-site attenuation or a soakaway rather than a straight connection. This is a design question, not a gutter question, but it is worth knowing before the downpipe is moved.
